# Ideas for Hearth and Host (Fable, 2026-11-28)

Stage 1 of the Fable push: ideas only, nothing built from them yet. Revised after the two scouts reported (their reports: `scout-late-report-2026-11-28.md` and `scout-new-report-2026-11-28.md` in the orchestrator's notes folder). Section 7 is the proposed build order.

Every idea is judged by the creator's five tests:

1. **Chunky:** a few big decisions, not many small clicks.
2. **Real trade-offs:** both answers can be right.
3. **Always engaging:** no dead stretch from minute 1 to the crown.
4. **Tension remains:** being strong never means being safe.
5. **The next thing looks fun:** you can always see something you want and can't do yet.

## 1. What the game is when it's good

From the creator's 2-hour playtest and the earlier ones, the moments that landed share one shape: **the world shows you a wall, and you build your way over it.**

- A raid you can't stop → the barracks, just in time.
- A double-walled city you can't take → the siege workshop.
- Advanced troops you can't arm → fixing the leather chain.
- A host you can't beat → the arrière-ban, mercenaries, running away.

The player never needs a quest list for these. The wall is visible, the answer takes thought and time, and getting over it changes what you can do. The slow marches and the slow battles work for the same reason: they make each commitment big.

Where the game sags, it's the opposite shape:

- **A threshold instead of a wall.** Ranks are a count of places (2, 5, 11). Peace is a number crossing a line. The crown is "hold half the realm". Nothing is seen, struggled with and beaten.
- **A click instead of a decision.** Hunting down every tannery. Four different buttons that all mean "raise more men". Numbers you have to go looking for.
- **Strength that makes you safe.** At minute 77 the creator was "way stronger than everyone on the map", and nothing punished an empty border.

So the direction is: **more walls, fewer thresholds; more decisions, fewer clicks; and a realm too wide to be safe everywhere.**

## 2. The big moves

### 2.1 Wars with a shape: a war score you can spend

**The problem.** Dunmore took peace after one town. Varn bent the knee before the creator could fight him: "kind of wish I got to play out the rest of the actual war". A war today is a few events and then a yes-or-no.

**The idea.** The game already keeps a hidden war score for each house (a battle won +10, a place taken +25, and so on). Make it the visible heart of every war:

- **A tug-of-war bar for each war,** on the house's panel and in a small war banner on the map. It says what moved it: "Rookdale taken +25", "your raiders beaten −8".
- **Peace has a price list.** When you offer peace, you pick what you ask for, and each term needs enough score:
  - white peace: they're tired, or you're level;
  - tribute in gold;
  - a named place, either one you hold or one you're besieging;
  - the knee: only when their host has been beaten in the field, or their seat is taken or under siege.
- **They ask too.** A house that's ahead demands terms from you: a place back, gold, or your word not to attack for a time. Losing a war becomes a negotiation too, not just a list of lost towns.
- **Strength alone never ends a war.** A far weaker house sues for a plain peace. It doesn't kneel to an army it hasn't met. This replaces the two separate fixes (playtest items 15 and 27) with one system.
- **The same rules between houses,** so their wars end in ceded towns and vassal houses for the same reasons yours do.

**Why it serves the tests.** Every battle and every town moves a bar you can cash in (chunky). "Take the gold now, or press on for the town and risk the score?" is a real trade-off. A war becomes a story with a middle.

**Cost:** medium to large. The score exists; the terms, the house's own demands, the interface and the house-to-house version are new. **Risk:** balance. Terms that are too cheap turn wars into a shop.

**Decision for the creator:** yes or no to the direction. I recommend yes; it's the biggest single improvement to the part of the game they most wanted more of.

### 2.2 A realm too wide to be safe: houses that pounce, and that you can see coming

**The problem.** The creator stripped one border to attack Dunmore and asked whether Varn would notice. Today Varn might, on a 4-to-8-minute clock, and only by counting the men standing inside each place. Being strongest overall makes you safe everywhere.

**The idea.**
- **Houses read openings.** A place's strength, as a neighbour reckons it, includes field armies near enough to relieve it and ignores armies too far to get back in time. When your host marches away, the houses that border the emptied side think again soon after, not on their slow clock.
- **You see the look before the blow.** A warning a minute or two ahead, with the house's name and the place: "Varn's outriders are counting the men at Stowe." Watchtowers and scouts make it earlier or surer. Now it's a decision: turn the army round, call that village's ban, pay Varn off, or gamble.
- **Raids go for the weak place,** not a random one.
- **The same rules for everyone:** a house that empties its border to attack you is open to its other neighbours, and to you.

**Why it serves the tests.** This is the "your attention is the scarce resource" hook made real, and it keeps tension when you're strong: you can win anywhere, but not everywhere at once. Slow marches (which the creator loves) are what make it bite. It also makes garrisons, walls, watchtowers and a ban you can call from one village (item 17) matter more.

**Cost:** medium. **Risk:** too twitchy and it feels unfair; the warning is what keeps it fair. This is a feel question, so I'd build it with a dial and let the creator try gentle against sharp.

**Decision for the creator:** how sharp. I recommend sharp with a clear warning.

### 2.3 One muster instead of four calls

**The problem.** "Raise more men" is at least four separate buttons with separate rules: call the banners (village levies and vassals' share), call the arrière-ban, a vassal's call to arms, and a sworn house's host. The creator asked for two more controls on top (send the ban home early; call it from one village or all).

**The idea.** One **Muster** sheet, opened from any place or army:
- One row per source: your villages' levies, the countryside (the ban, per place, with each place's own timer), your vassals, sworn houses.
- Each row shows men, cost in gold and food, how long they serve, and when they could arrive.
- Tick what you want, pick where they gather, and see the total against your army cap before you commit.
- The same sheet sends the ban home early, place by place.

**Why it serves the tests.** One big decision ("how large a host, at what price, from where") replaces several small ones, and the creator's two ban requests fall out of it without two more buttons. It also scales: at Duke it's the same sheet with more rows.

**Cost:** medium. Mostly interface over rules that exist; the ban's timer becomes per place. **Risk:** low.

**Decision for the creator:** none needed unless they object. It delivers items 16 and 17.

### 2.4 Rank-ups as moments: a charter at each rank

**The problem.** "I really liked becoming a lord" is one of the strongest things the creator has said, yet a rank is only a count of places, and each rank-up is a herald card. The coronation is the one rank-up with a choice, and it's the best one.

**The idea.** At each new rank the herald offers **one of three charters**, kept for the rest of the campaign. They are ways of ruling, each a real lean, not a flat bonus. For example:
- **Lord:**
  - *Marcher lord:* the countryside sends more men and serves longer.
  - *Steward of the roads:* a second wagon in every village and faster hauling.
  - *Castellan:* walls and keeps cost less and masons come free.
- **Count:**
  - *Lord of the tourney:* veterans train cheaper and elites draw less pay.
  - *Merchant prince:* markets earn more and companies hire cheaper.
  - *Hammer of outlaws:* safe roads, a happier realm and more newcomers.
- **Duke:**
  - *Kingmaker:* houses warm to you and vassals send more.
  - *Iron duke:* a bigger army cap and cheaper siege engines.
  - *Builder of cities:* your seat grows a level beyond its limit.

The next rank's three charters show greyed on the realm panel, so there is always something to look forward to.

**Why it serves the tests.** One chunky, permanent, satisfying choice per rank; a different campaign each time; a visible "next thing". It also prepares V2: in "Heroes" your character's own skills can sit beside the house's charters.

**Cost:** small to medium for each charter; the design care is in making all three tempting. **Risk:** a best pick emerges and the choice goes flat. Rival houses should take charters too (same rules), which also gives each house more character.

**Decision for the creator:** yes or no, and whether the examples have the right flavour. I recommend yes.

### 2.5 Great hosts on the map: threats you avoid first and beat later

**The problem.** Between the early raids and the late league, most of what threatens you is sized to you. The creator's favourite scare was the exception: Norhold reinforcing a camp with 1,331 men, "very Pendor-esque… scale getting injected".

**The idea.** A few **named great hosts** that roam on their own business from the midgame:
- an outlaw king gathering the camps;
- a free company gone rogue for want of an employer;
- a northern warband come early over the wall.

Each is too big to fight when it appears, moves slowly and visibly, takes what's weak in its path (yours or a rival's), and can be shadowed, paid off, baited onto an enemy, or beaten later for a real prize: its plunder, its captain as a vassal, or renown with every house. They follow the game's rules: they eat, they're paid, and they can starve.

**Why it serves the tests.** A wall you can see from far off (test 5), tension that doesn't depend on a house's mood (test 4), and a chunky choice each time one comes near (tests 1 and 2). It also foreshadows the north.

**Cost:** medium. Armies, captains, outlaw camps and companies exist; the roaming plan, the scaling and the prizes are new. **Risk:** unfair deaths early. They must be slow, announced, and avoidable at a price.

**Decision for the creator:** yes or no. I recommend yes, starting with one (the outlaw king).

### 2.6 The crown costs deeds

**The evidence.** The late-game scout was crowned from the Duke test world **in five clicks and 300 gold, with no game time passing**: gift a house, ask it to back your claim, repeat. In the war route, Varn offered to kneel three minutes after war was declared, before a blow was struck, and then five more times. The scout: "I was never in danger of losing… A war is won by declaring it and waiting for the offer to kneel." No league formed, because the houses couldn't pay for one.

**The idea.** The top of the climb becomes the biggest wall in the game, in three parts:

- **Press your claim** (from Duke) is a moment the player starts. Each house then answers in its own voice: back you, stand aside, or stand against you. Each names its **price, and it is a deed, never a gift**: beat its host in the field, win a war for it against the house it hates, marry into it and keep that peace, return a town it claims, or hold its seat. Gold can sweeten a deed but not replace one.
- **The houses that stand against you league at once and fight as one.** The league no longer waits on a count of places or on its members' purses: declaring for the crown is the reason, and each member's levy for it is free that one time, as yours is at your coronation muster. Its one great host is the set-piece battle of Part 1, the thousand-a-side field the game was built for.
- **Kingsbridge is a coronation siege.** Take it and hold it for a set time while everyone who hasn't backed you comes for it. The crown is the moment the last of them breaks or kneels.

Pressing early catches the houses unready but leaves you thin; pressing late is the reverse. That choice of timing is the player's.

**Why it serves the tests.** The crown becomes the last and tallest wall, seen from far off and climbed in the player's own order, by war, marriage and favour in whatever mix they like (tests 1, 2 and 5). It closes the five-click crown.

**Cost:** large. It reuses the backing, league, host and coronation code, and builds on 2.1's war score. **Risk:** the deeds must be readable and fair; a house whose price can't be met needs a way round (beat it).

**Decision for the creator:** yes or no to the shape. I recommend yes. It is the single most important late-game change.

### 2.7 The north as a host you can see

**The evidence.** In the King test, 8,641 northerners spent their time marching in loops between their own places while the Gate of Kolnes stood guarded by **6 men**. Their hosts never reached the player's heartland. Waves scale with the player's strength, a hidden rubber band of the kind the creator has ruled out. Nothing on screen says how the north is beaten: the goals panel reads "9 of 9". About half the session went on assembling and walking.

**The idea.**
- **One named host per wave.** It comes out of the Gate on a drawn road, toward a named place of yours, with a countdown: "The host of Jarl Hrolf, about 1,400, marches on Sedgeley: 6 minutes." You choose to meet it in the field, hold a wall against it, or give the place up and strike its camp while it's away.
- **A fixed, rising schedule,** not one that follows your strength. The waves are known to grow; how far you get ahead of them is your doing.
- **The Gate always keeps a real guard,** and taking the war camps one by one visibly weakens the next wave. The on-screen goal says how many camps stand and what the next wave will be.
- **The north is heard before the crown** (from Count): refugees with stories, a winter warband over the wall, and the choice to mend the old wall before you need it. The shatter stays a shock in scale, not in subject.
- **Sworn houses fight as part of your host** when called (their men join your muster), not as 60-man armies sent alone against camps of 300.

**Why it serves the tests.** It turns Part 2 from a fog of columns into a run of visible walls, each a chunky choice, with rising tension and a clear end.

**Cost:** large. **Risk:** balance of the schedule, which wants a sandbox to tune.

**Decision for the creator:** yes or no. I recommend yes, built after 2.6, since the creator's next playthrough reaches the crown first.

### 2.10 Battles at the size the game was built for

**The evidence.** The goal is 1,200-man battles at Count and Duke. The late-game scout saw **no fight with 300 men on both sides**. The 600-man arrière-ban met 26 men. Banners arrived as 9 to 11 separate columns, many of 12 men, each dissolving into a garrison; the army dissolved again into every village it took; and the ban's 13 minutes were mostly spent walking to the muster.

**The idea.** Big battles need both sides to arrive whole and to want the fight:
- **One muster makes one army** (2.3), with an arrival time shown, and it **stays an army** after it takes a place, leaving only the garrison you choose.
- **The ban's clock starts when it reaches the muster,** not when it's called.
- **Hosts seek the decisive battle.** A house's great host, the league's host and each northern wave march as one body on a named target with a warning, and a host that meets yours on the road offers battle rather than slipping past. A war's biggest swing in score (2.1) is beating the enemy's host, so both sides want that field.
- **Trivial fights resolve themselves** (being built with the bug fixes), so the fights you're asked about are the ones worth fighting.

**Cost:** medium, mostly inside 2.1, 2.3, 2.6 and 2.7. **Risk:** low.

### 2.11 Each rank takes the old chores away

**The evidence.** At Duke the "Needs you" list was still "N without work" and "quarry worked out" for villages a governor runs. Event cards about a mine fall or a 60-gold request came every few minutes "at the same size as when you were a reeve". Every such choice had an obvious answer, because the purse held 11,000 to 33,000 gold.

**The idea.** The design already says each rank should relieve the rank before. Make it a rule of the interface:
- **Your steward answers small things from Count.** Village troubles below a size are dealt with by the sensible cheap choice and written in the chronicle ("Your steward had the miners at Oakley dug out: 40 wood"). They reach you only at your seat, or when they're big.
- **Needs you is ranked by rank.** A duke is told of hosts, sieges, risings and empty treasuries, not of idle woodcutters two villages away.
- **Events grow with you.** A duke's events are a duke's: a vassal's rebellion brewing, a house offering a match, a famine across a province, a great host on the border.
- **Gold has realm-sized uses** so the choices stop being flat: a standing host that draws real wages, fortified passes, the deeds of 2.6, the wall in the north.

**Cost:** medium. **Risk:** hiding something the player wanted to see; the chronicle and a "let me decide these" switch at the keep cover it.

**Decision for the creator:** none needed; it carries out a rule already in the design.

### 2.9 A limit on what you can rule by hand (downgraded: watch, don't build)

**Corrected evidence.** My first read was wrong. The creator ran **five** places by hand; Varn's submission took the realm to about ten, and "I was at the point where I would start needing vassals". So the game's own weight brought them to the edge of delegating at about five places, at the rank of Count, which is where the design wants it.

**What that means.** A hard-coded limit on places ruled in person isn't needed on this evidence. Keep the idea in reserve: if later runs show a player comfortably tending eight or ten places by hand and never granting land, add a soft limit then (worse tax and slower building beyond the rank's number, shown before you take a place, with the grant one click away).

**What to do instead now.** Make the step into vassals inviting at the moment it's needed: when you take your fifth or sixth place, the capture card offers "Rule it yourself" beside "Grant it to…" with what each gives you. That is small, and it belongs with the muster and the steward's word.

### 2.8 The steward's word: name what's holding a village back

**The problem.** The creator enjoys the economy see-saw ("the fact that I have to put in this thought is good") but keeps hunting for causes: why newcomers stopped, where the leather went, how many veterans there are.

**The idea.** One line per village, and one for the realm, that names the single thing holding it back right now, in plain words and from the game's own numbers: "Short of leather: three tanners are making armour." "No room: 34 of 34 beds." "Food is at the farms, not the stores: one more storehouse hand." Click it and it shows you the buildings concerned. It never fixes anything for you.

**Why it serves the tests.** It keeps the thinking and removes the hunting. The decided fixes (the newcomers line, the veteran count, default recipes per village) are pieces of this; the steward's word is the whole.

**Cost:** medium. **Risk:** if it's wrong once, the player stops trusting it, so it needs checks.

**Decision for the creator:** none needed; it extends what they've already asked for.

## 3. Smaller additions

- **Custom shipments** (decided): one good, any amount, in wagon-loads.
- **Default recipes per village** (decided), and no derived goods by default.
- **Levies count the keep and storehouses** (decided).
- **Winter: make the fallow winter the default** (the creator is leaning this way, 2026-11-28). They barely felt the mild winter: "it affected me early on, but eventually i built a good reserve of food then it wasn't an issue", and then: "im leaning towards fallow winters bc that way i would feel it even if i was prepared". Fallow is already built (`?winter=hard`): fields rest in winter and their hands haul; spring sows at 0.6, summer 1.0, autumn 1.6, and a year's food is unchanged. With a reserve you are safe, but you still watch the larder fall all winter and plan the year around the harvest. Flip the default for the next playthrough and keep mild behind `?winter=mild`. The houses' stewards already lay in for it.
- **A scout report that names the wall:** when you look at a place you can't take, the report says what would take it ("stone walls: bring a ram or ladders, or starve it in about 9 minutes"), so the next goal is always visible.

## 4. Cuts and merges to consider

- **Four calls into one muster** (2.3).
- **The league forming by itself** becomes the player's "press your claim" (2.6), if the scout's report supports it.
- **Vassals' fine detail** (wants, favours, knacks, petitions, titles, marriages, claims, factions). There's a great deal of it and the creator hasn't reached it: they ran every place by hand to nearly Duke (see 2.9). So it is unjudged, not unwanted. Cut nothing here until someone has played it; the scout's report and 2.9 come first.
- **Events every 7 to 12 minutes:** check that they add decisions and don't just interrupt.
- **Considered and not recommended now: a "trade" for each village** (farming, arms, timber and iron), as rival places have. The creator weighed production focuses and chose village defaults. Revisit only if ten villages prove too many to tend.

## 5. The first session (from the new-player scout)

The creator's sister will meet the game through the first-campaign defaults. The scout played them as a newcomer for 24 minutes.

- **No threat ever came.** The first campaign defaults to Gentle, which switches the outlaws off, while the opening text still says "when the outlaws come". The scout kept the retinue at home for 13 minutes waiting. This is the flat opening the design set out to fix. **Recommendation:** the first campaign gets one small, announced outlaw band at about minute 5, sized to the retinue, even on Gentle. It gives the first "I held" moment. (The creator's call.)
- **The best moment was one nobody pointed at:** taking the first free village at minute 14 (the fight, the rank of Lord, the new screen). **Recommendation:** point at the first neighbour by minute 6 to 8, with its strength shown before any march, as an optional goal.
- **The worst confusion was wood:** three different numbers for one good. Being fixed now (one honest number).
- **The goal isn't on screen in the village,** where the first 15 minutes happen. Being fixed now.
- **Trackpad:** no way to pan in the village without keys, and orders need right-click. Being fixed now.
- **A dead four minutes at 17 to 21** with gold piling up. **Recommendation:** the first house makes itself known in that gap: an envoy, a demand or an offer, so the wider game arrives while the player is still curious.
- **The odds card said "about 3 in 10" for a storm that was won easily.** The auto-resolve estimate and a storm fought by hand disagree; worth a look so the card can be trusted.

## 5b. Still unknown

- **A real Duke-level game.** The test worlds hand out places, gold and Kingsbridge, and leave the houses poor, so "no tension at Duke" is likely but unproven. The creator's own finished run is the best evidence there is: an exported copy of that save would let a scout play on from it.
- **Why the test bots climb so slowly** when the creator reached Duke in about 2 hours.

## 6. What to protect

Slow combat and slow marches. The cost of war as men away from work. The merc price ladder and the company cap. The economy see-saw and readable shortages. Becoming a Lord and town templates. Towns that yield to a strong host. Walled cities that demand a siege train. The arrière-ban answer to a raid. The gathering-host warning. Rivals on the player's rules, with no AI-only caps.

## 7. Proposed build order

Each batch ends with the game played, checked and refreshed for the creator.

1. **Now building (bugs and clarity):** the playtest fixes, the first-session fixes, the late-game bugs, then a QC pass.
2. **Wars and hosts:** the war score and terms (2.1), no kneeling before a battle, one muster that makes one army with the ban's changes (2.3, 2.10), raiders that go for the weak place and houses that pounce with a warning (2.2).
3. **The economy the creator asked for:** default recipes per village and no derived goods by default, custom shipments, levies that count the keep, the fallow winter as the default, the steward's word (2.8), the grant offer on the capture card (2.9).
4. **The crown and the north:** deeds for backing, pressing the claim, the league's host, the coronation siege (2.6); then the north as visible hosts (2.7); the old chores falling away (2.11).
5. **Delight:** a charter at each rank (2.4), the first great host (2.5), the first-session threat and the early neighbour (5).

Still the creator's to weigh: right of passage through other houses' lands (playtest item 22). With 2.2 in, a host crossing your land is already a warning you see and can answer; I'd judge passage after playing that.
